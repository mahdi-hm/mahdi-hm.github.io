What I'm sharing is no secret to anyone at this point. It's even getting a bit boring. Like
everyone, I've heard the rewrite stories. But working with agents on my own team was shocking,
and honestly, a little scary.

It wasn't only code. Even in ops and in framing customer issues, the agents were great. The
amount of code we pushed went up a lot, and now we can see, and actually feel, that a big change
is very close.

Khabib once said, "I take people into deep waters, and they discover themselves." I believe AI
is doing the same with us, and that's why I'm optimistic and excited, while equally fearful.

## The flood

Scary, of course, because of what it means for the software industry, and for being a software
engineer or an engineering manager. But on another level, it's the flood: of PRs, of code, and
of issues we might not even be able to point to.

As a manager, it adds another layer. How can I tell who is delivering good quality and who
isn't, if everyone uses the same model? How can anyone find time to read all the deliverables?
What about performance reviews? How do I assess the work at all?

And above all: are we adding value for our customers? Are we shipping software with fewer bugs,
or are we just shipping bugs, more and faster?

## What's actually scarce now

**Saying precisely what must hold.** We used to write a design doc, get it approved, and then
implement it. That's no longer enough. The gap between the design and the code, now mostly
generated by agents, keeps widening. Slow coding had a side effect: it gave us time to
internalise the design, think it through, and iterate when needed. Now coding can sometimes be
done 100 times faster. So we have to put that time into describing what we want, or what we
*really* want. I'd go further: we should explain why what we're asking for is what we really
need, much like avoiding the [XY problem](https://xyproblem.info/). The bottleneck now is saying
precisely what needs to be done.

**Judging whether a change is right.** Nobody reads code like in the old days. People use
agents, and I don't blame them. I even encourage it. But some tension has come up. Some people
see AI slop as disrespectful, and I understand them: if they read your code, they expect you to
have put at least the same amount of time into writing it.

**Tests that can be trusted.** We used to spend time writing tests. Now an agent can do it, but
can those tests be trusted? How do we know tests generated by the same machinery aren't there
just to pass? We need tests strong enough to check that what we get is what we wanted. Call it
WYSIWYA: What You See Is What You Asked.

**A record of what was checked.** I believe the way PRs are created needs to change. Maybe each
one should have a customer-experience section that lists the rules the change must follow, like
no duplicate writes.

## Where I'm looking

On the customer side, I believe ticket MTTR should be one way to measure success. In the long
run, though, fewer tickets must be the goal.

When I read the paper on how the Cedar team built their authorization language
([*How We Built Cedar: A Verification-Guided Approach*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01688)),
I realised some teams are genuinely adapting to the new norm and finding ways to ship reliable
software. At the same time, their approach may not fully apply to my work.

I do believe we need a dam: something to control the flood, or even to get energy out of it.

I'm taking this journey with you. We shouldn't use agents blindly, or just because we can, at
least where there's no room for error. If you're building a database, the least you should
guarantee is that you never lose your customers' data. I'll share my thoughts and experience in
the coming posts, but with agents, we should aim for outcomes we can check.
